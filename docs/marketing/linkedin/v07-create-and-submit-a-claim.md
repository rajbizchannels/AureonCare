```
Status is the whole story

Billing ▸ Claims is the money pipeline: every claim the practice has, and where each one has got to. Four statuses carry almost all the meaning.

Draft — built but not sent. Nothing has left the building.

Submitted — gone to the payer, waiting on an answer.

Paid — settled.

Denied — needs work.

A billing team's whole job can be described as moving things leftward out of Denied and rightward into Paid, and the queue is designed so you can see at a glance where the money has stalled. If you only ever learn one screen in the billing module, learn how to read this one by status.
```

**→ Insert `assets/v07/03-what-and-why.png` here.**

```
Building the claim, without retyping anything

Pick the patient and the claim inherits their insurance. Nobody re-keys a policy number, which matters because a policy number is exactly the kind of field that is both long and unverifiable at the point of entry — if you mistype it, nothing complains until the payer does. This is also where the registration habits from issue 02 pay off: the insurance captured properly at the front desk is the insurance that arrives here.

Service date and charge come from the visit you are billing for.

Then the coding, which is the part that actually requires a person.

Diagnosis codes are searched, not remembered. Type the code if you know it, or type the words if you do not, and pick from the list. The same for the procedure.

And the relationship between those two is the thing to hold on to:

The procedure code is what you are billing. The diagnosis is why.

Payers check that the two agree. A procedure that the diagnosis does not account for is the most ordinary reason a clean-looking claim comes back denied — not fraud, not incompetence, just a pair that does not explain each other to someone who was not there.
```

**→ Insert `assets/v07/04-draft-then-send.png` here.**

```
Nothing leaves until you send it

The claim is created as a draft. Nothing has been transmitted, and drafts are safe to fix — which is worth saying out loud, because the fear of sending something wrong is what makes people slow on this screen. You can build it, look at it, and change your mind.

When it is right, Submit EDI 837 sends it to the clearinghouse in the format payers expect. EDI 837 is simply the standard electronic claim format; the point is that you are not assembling it by hand or posting anything.

From there it comes back paid, or denied with a reason. A denial goes straight to the Denials queue, where it becomes a piece of work with an explanation attached rather than a silence you have to go looking for.
```

Why this one is worth watching even if you do not bill

Billing is the only part of a practice where the quality of everyone else's work shows up as a number.

The claim inherits the insurance captured at registration. Its service date comes from the appointment someone booked. Its diagnosis comes from what a clinician recorded. By the time a biller opens the claim, most of its accuracy has already been decided by people who were not thinking about billing at all.

That is the argument for watching this one as a whole practice rather than leaving it to the billing desk: it is where you see what the rest of the series was quietly for.
