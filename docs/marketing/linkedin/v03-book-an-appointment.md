Every booking answers four questions

Scheduling ▸ Calendar is the practice diary — every provider, every room. New Appointment opens a form that is really just four questions.

Who. Start typing, or pick from the register. The MRN is shown alongside the name, which is how you tell apart the two patients who share a surname before you book the wrong one.

What. The appointment type.

With whom. Only providers who actually work that day are offered, so you cannot book a clinician who is not in.

When. Pick the slot. The duration follows from the type you chose.

AureonCare confirms before it writes, so a mis-click never books a patient.

```
Change the type, not the clock

This is the whole issue in five words, and it is the habit worth teaching a new starter on day one.

The appointment type is not a label. It sets the duration, and it marks whether the visit is virtual. A follow-up and a new-patient consultation are different lengths because they are different kinds of work, and the calendar already knows that.

So when a booking does not fit, the answer is to change the type — not to stretch the clock by hand. Overriding the duration gets that one appointment into the diary and teaches the calendar something that is not true. Do it often enough and the practice loses the ability to answer simple questions: how long do follow-ups actually take, how many can we fit on a Thursday, are we over-running because of the schedule or in spite of it.

The video shows the duration change as the type changes, which is worth seeing once — it makes the rule obvious in a way that reading about it does not:

https://youtu.be/faMT4PZbVsY
```

**→ Insert `assets/v03/04-three-views.png` here.**

```
The same diary, three ways

Day view is for working the desk hour by hour. Week view is for spotting the gaps worth filling. List view is the same diary as a searchable table, and it is where you go to change or cancel a booking.

Three views, one set of appointments. Nothing is filed anywhere different depending on how you looked at it.

And one word matters here more than any other in this issue: cancel. Not delete.

Cancelling keeps the record and the reason it did not happen. Deleting erases the history, and with it any way of answering why a slot went empty, how often a particular patient does not attend, or whether the practice has a Monday-morning problem. The booking that never happened is still information. Cancel it, do not delete it.
```

## Sourcing

Every claim in the body traces to the video or its script: Scheduling ▸ Calendar
as the practice diary, day and week views and what each is for, the four
questions a booking answers, the MRN disambiguating namesakes, the type setting
duration and marking a visit virtual, only that day's providers being offered,
the one-line reason the clinician reads, the confirmation step, the List view as
a searchable table, and cancelling rather than deleting to keep the record and
its reason. The 2:24 runtime and the position as video 3 of 8 come from the
metadata file.

The opening — a diary drifting over about six months — is written as an
illustration, not a measured finding, and the closing argument about losing the
ability to answer "how long do follow-ups actually take" is reasoning from the
product's behaviour rather than a claim about any practice's data. No statistics
are quoted, because none in the source material are ours to quote.
