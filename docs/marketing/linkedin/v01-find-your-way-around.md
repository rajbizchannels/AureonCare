### Body

Paste as-is. LinkedIn's editor keeps bold and headings; it does not keep

```
Nobody reads the manual. They ask the person sitting next to them.

That is how practice software actually gets learned. A new front-desk hire starts on Monday, and by Wednesday they know the four screens they need because someone showed them. It works — right up until the person next to them is with a patient, or on leave, or has just left.

The fix is not more documentation. It is a navigation model small enough to hold in your head on day one. AureonCare has one, and it takes about two minutes to learn.
```

**→ Insert `assets/02-three-panes.png` here.**

```
Every screen is the same three panes

Wherever you are in AureonCare, the screen is divided the same way.

Pane 1 is the workspace rail down the left: Home, Scheduling, Patients, Clinical, Billing, and the rest, with Settings at the bottom. It does not change.

Pane 2 lists the modules inside whichever workspace you picked. Choose Scheduling and you get Calendar, Appointments and the scheduling tools. Choose Patients and you get Electronic Health Records, Patient History and the rest.

Pane 3 is the work itself — the calendar you are booking into, the chart you are writing, the claim you are submitting.

That is the whole model. And once you have it, every instruction anyone gives you collapses into two words: Scheduling ▸ Calendar. Patients ▸ Electronic Health Records. Billing ▸ Claims.

It sounds like a small thing. It is the difference between "click the thing at the top, no, the other one" and a direction you can text to a colleague.
```

**→ Insert `assets/03-universal-search.png` here.**

```
When you already know what you want, stop navigating

Clicking through panes is for exploring. When you know the patient's name or the claim you are chasing, use the magnifier in the top bar instead.

One box covers patients, appointments, claims and tasks. Type "Sarah" and the results come back grouped by what they are, so a patient record is never buried in a list of appointments. Pick one and you land directly on that record.

Two clicks, from anywhere in the product, to anything in the practice. For a front desk with a patient on the phone, that is the feature that matters most — and it is the one new users most often do not know is there.
```

**→ Insert `assets/04-top-bar.png` here.**

```
Three tools that never move

The top bar stays put no matter which workspace you are in, and it carries three things worth knowing on day one.

The bell collects what needs a human: denied claims, portal messages from patients, payments that have posted. It is the closest thing the product has to an inbox for the practice.

Help opens the guides for the module you are standing in — not a generic help centre, the page for the screen in front of you.

The theme switch flips the whole workspace between dark and light. A front desk under bright reception lighting and a clinician in a dim exam room do not want the same screen, and neither of them should have to put up with the other's.

The AI assistant sits in the same bar. Same place, every screen.
```

**→ Insert `assets/05-recap.png` here.**

```
Why we made this two minutes long

Onboarding cost is not paid once. It is paid per person, forever — every new hire, every locum covering a week, every practice manager stepping onto reception because someone called in sick.

So the first video in our Getting Started series does not tour features. It teaches the model: three panes, one search box, a top bar that does not move. Two minutes, with chapters and proper subtitles, so it can be watched at a desk with the sound off before a shift starts.

It is video 1 of 8. The other seven cover registering a patient, booking an appointment, reading your day on the dashboard, documenting a visit, running a telehealth call, creating and submitting a claim, and what your patients see in their own portal. Under eighteen minutes for the set.

Where to start, by role:

• Front desk — 1, 2, 3, then 8
• Clinician — 1, 4, 5, 6
• Billing — 1, then 7
• Practice manager — 1 and 4, then whichever apply

Watch video 1 first regardless. Every later video gives directions in the form "go to Scheduling, then Calendar" and does not stop to explain what that means.

Everything in the series is recorded in a demo environment with synthetic data. Every patient, clinician, appointment and claim in it is invented.

Next issue: registering a new patient — which fields matter later, and which ones quietly cost you a denied claim three weeks on.
```
