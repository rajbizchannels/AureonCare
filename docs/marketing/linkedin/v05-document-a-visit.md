```
Read the chart before you write in it

Patients ▸ Patient History is the longitudinal chart — every visit this patient has ever had, not just the one in front of you. Opening a patient gives you the whole record on one set of tabs, and three of them are worth reading before you touch the keyboard.

Patient Chart carries demographics, medical history and allergies.

Diagnoses is the active problem list — what this patient is actually being treated for, as opposed to what they came in about today.

Records holds the notes from previous visits, newest first. Thirty seconds here is usually the difference between a consultation that starts from scratch and one that starts from where the last one finished.

None of this is new information to a clinician. What is worth saying is that it is all on one set of tabs, in one place, without opening a second system or waiting for notes to be faxed over.
```

```
Read the allergies first. Always.

It is one line in the video and it is the most important one.

Allergies sit on the Patient Chart tab alongside demographics and medical history. They are the first thing to look at, and the last thing anyone wants to discover afterwards. Putting them on the same tab as the demographics is deliberate — it means you pass them on the way in, every time, rather than having to remember to go and look.

Everything else in this issue is about efficiency. This part is not.
```

**→ Insert `assets/v05/04-two-readers.png` here.**

```
Recording what you found

Edit Patient Chart is where today's findings go, grouped into tabs.

Physical takes the measurements you just did — and they chart over time automatically, so a blood pressure recorded today is not just a number in a note, it is the newest point on a line somebody can look at in six months.

Allergies and medical history live here too. Anything you add becomes what every other clinician sees at the next visit, which is the real argument for recording it at the time rather than at the end of the clinic.

Then you save, and two things happen at once.

The care team sees it. One record, visible immediately to everyone treating this patient — not a note that syncs overnight or lives in your drafts until you sign off the day.

And the patient sees it. Records, diagnoses and prescriptions appear in their portal.
```

```
Write for both readers

That second audience is the part worth sitting with, because it changes what good documentation looks like.

A note written only for colleagues can be accurate and still land badly with the person it describes. A note written only for the patient can be warm and clinically useless. The job is one piece of text that does both: precise enough for the next clinician, plain enough that the patient reading it on their phone that evening understands what happened and what comes next.

In practice that is a small set of habits. Expand the abbreviation the first time you use it. Say what the finding means, not only what it was. If the plan depends on the patient doing something, write the plan where they will see it rather than assuming reception will relay it.

None of that takes longer. It mostly takes knowing, while you type, that there are two people at the other end.

The full walkthrough — opening the chart, reading before you write, recording measurements and history, and what appears in the portal afterwards — is here:

[ VIDEO 5 LINK ]

It has chapters and a proper subtitle track.

This is video 5 of 8 in the AureonCare Getting Started series. Videos 1 to 4 cover navigation, registering a patient, booking them in and reading your day; 6, 7 and 8 cover telehealth, claims and the portal from the patient's own side.

Everything in the series is recorded in a demo environment with synthetic data. Every patient, measurement and note in it is invented.

Next issue: running a telehealth visit — from an appointment through to joining the call.
```
